# Dairy Queen Dairy-Free Market Entry: Analytics and Go-to-Market

A two-stage case study on whether, and how, Dairy Queen should launch a dairy-free Blizzard. Stage one is a five-person graduate class analytics study I led. Stage two is my independent product marketing recommendation, which revised the team's pricing, market sizing, and rollout after pressure-testing them against cost, franchise, and competitive realities.

By Naveen Raj Kanagaraj, MS in Marketing Analysis, DePaul University.

This is an academic and independent case study. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by Dairy Queen. Product visuals in the deck are concept mockups, unit economics are illustrative, and third-party figures are cited below.

**[Read the go-to-market deck (PDF)](reports/DQ-Dairy-Free-PMM.pdf)**

## The recommendation

Launch a dairy-free Blizzard as a **premium line extension**, priced about $1 above the classic, through a **6-month opt-in pilot** with ~30 franchisees in three candidate metros. Scale only if dairy-free mix reaches at least 8% of Blizzard orders, repeat reaches at least 30% among triers, and the pilot shows net-new group visits.

**Why:**

- **The category is growing.** North America non-dairy ice cream is estimated at $0.86B in 2025, growing 6.7% a year to 2030 (Mordor Intelligence).
- **DQ needs a growth catalyst.** U.S. stores fell by 42 in 2024, to 4,212, with flat average unit volume of about $1.165M (QSR Magazine).
- **The QSR counter is open.** McDonald's, Wendy's, and Culver's offer no dairy-free blended treat. DQ's only dairy-free item is the Non-Dairy Dilly Bar.
- **Demand must be proven.** Shake Shack added non-dairy frozen custard to its core menu in 2023 and later discontinued it, which is why this is a pilot with kill criteria, not a chain-wide launch.
- **DQ cannot mandate.** DQ is nearly 100% franchised, so franchisees opt in based on equipment, training, and margin.

## Stage one: the team analytics study

**Methods:** Perceptual Mapping · Conjoint Analysis · Value Curve & Kano Model · Structured Problem Framing · Survey Design

**My role.** I led this five-person team over roughly three months, from problem framing and survey design through analysis and the final team recommendation. I owned the quantitative core:

- **Led the conjoint analysis end to end:** study design, the L18 orthogonal profile set, feature-importance decomposition, part-worth utility estimation, and the total-preference-utility model.
- **Co-led the value curve and Kano findings:** separated satisfaction drivers from table stakes.
- **Coordinated the team across all four methods.**

**Methods detail.**

1. **Perceptual mapping.** n = 52, 5 brands across 8 attributes, positioning DQ against Jeni's, Ben & Jerry's, Baskin-Robbins, and Andy's.

![Perceptual map, brands vs. purchase-driver attributes](charts/perceptual_map.png)

2. **Conjoint analysis.** n = 50, 7 attributes at 3 levels, L18 orthogonal design.

![Conjoint part-worth utilities](charts/conjoint_utilities.png)

3. **Value curve and Kano model.** Survey of Millennial and Gen Z consumers in urban U.S. markets.
4. **Structured problem framing (PLCN).** 40 consumer pain points across 7 categories.

**Key findings.**

- **DQ is poorly positioned today.** On the perceptual map, DQ sits near the origin, in the low-consideration, under-differentiated space.
- **Price is the top purchase driver.** Attribute importance: price 21.97%, availability 20.62%, type of ice cream 19.41%. Certification (6.87%) and social-cause messaging (1.35%) matter little.
- **The preferred configuration is specific.** Top part-worth utilities: $4.99 price (0.221), indulgent flavor (0.183), oat-milk base (0.183), walk-in purchase (0.166).
- **There is headroom for DQ.** DQ's utility-maximizing configuration scored 1.065, versus Jeni's (0.856), Andy's (0.828), Ben & Jerry's (0.796), and Baskin-Robbins (0.743). This compares an optimized concept with competitors' existing products, so it shows headroom, not a forecast win.
- **Stated demand is strong.** 91.8% said environmental, health, or ethical factors influence food choices at least occasionally, ~80% would switch to or consider dairy-free, and ~55% would pay the same or only $1 more.
- **The gap to close is customization.** DQ leads on affordability and flavor but under-delivers on personalization.

**Team recommendation:** a dairy-free Blizzard at a $4.99 entry price on an oat-milk base, sold through DQ's walk-in stores, marketed on indulgence rather than certification or cause.

## Stage two: what I changed, and why

| Team study | Final recommendation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| $4.99 entry price | ~$1 premium over the classic, tested at $0.50 to $1.50 | The conjoint modeled demand only, not the oat-milk cost floor or franchise fees |
| $2.65B global category (2030 projection) | $0.86B North America (2025) | Size the market DQ actually serves, in the current year |
| Chain-wide rollout across 4,200+ stores | Opt-in franchise pilot with kill criteria | DQ is nearly 100% franchised, and Shake Shack's non-dairy custard was discontinued |

### Pricing and unit economics (illustrative)

| Line | Per medium Blizzard |
|---|---|
| Menu price (premium) | ~$6.29 |
| Cost of goods + ~9% royalty and ad fund | ~($2.47) |
| Oat-milk supplier premium | ~($0.40) |
| **Contribution** | **~$3.42 (~54%)** |

A customer who switches from a ~$5.29 classic adds only about $0.51 in contribution; a net-new customer adds the full ~$3.42. That is why incrementality, not trial, is the deciding pilot metric.

## Limitations

- Unit economics use estimated costs and an assumed ~120 Blizzards per store per day (about 20% of a $1.165M average unit volume). Blizzard prices are set by franchisees and vary by location.
- The opt-in pilot favors motivated franchisees, so results may overstate chain-wide performance. Matched control stores reduce, but do not remove, this bias.
- Candidate metros (Chicago, Atlanta, Phoenix) were chosen for DQ store density; plant-based demand in each still needs validation.
- The conjoint (n = 50) and perceptual map (n = 52) are small, directional class samples skewed toward urban Millennial and Gen Z consumers, and stated willingness to pay usually overstates real purchase behavior.
